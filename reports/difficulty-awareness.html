Do weaker judges sense when an item is hard for the expert?

Yes, from their own confidence on the answers they see. They can't tell from the question alone, and asking them directly about the expert adds nothing.

  1. 1A weaker judge's doubt flags the expert's errors even on items it gets right: AUROC on average, significant on 26 of 28 judge–benchmark points.
  2. 2When the weaker judge is itself wrong, its doubt says little: AUROC .
  3. 3The doubt points at capable judges' errors: it predicts the expert's errors slightly better than other weak judges' errors (+0.02, higher on 79% of points, p < 0.001).
  4. 4Asking directly doesn't help: from the question alone, predictions are at chance (). With the answers shown they work (), but slightly worse than plain confidence (, p = 0.001).
  5. 5The difficulty they sense is about the comparison: it tracks items where human annotators split, not whether the underlying question can be solved.
  6. 6The useful action is human review, not a bigger judge: holding back the 20% most-doubted items catches 43–61% of the expert's errors (29–33% on AlpacaFarm). On those items the expert drops to 0.61–0.72 and no other judge does better than 0.66, yet some judge is right 89–94% of the time. Nobody can tell which one.
For routing: doubt says an item is hard, not which judge is right, so a router built on it ties with the best single judge. Use it to decide which items go to a human. It can't predict difficulty from the question before the answers exist.

Caveats

  • One sample per verdict. Asking for confidence changed 12–20% of verdicts; confidence comes from that run, correctness from the stored run.
  • Own confidence averages both answer orders; the direct prediction is one call in one order, which favours confidence.
  • The expert is the most accurate judge on the full data. The same judges appear on every benchmark, so points aren't independent.
  • Selective-judging curves use the full data (no thresholds are fitted, but the expert is chosen in-sample).
  • Few expert errors where the weak judge is right (4–20% of 112–358 pairs), so per-point AUROCs are noisy.
  • Tinker no longer serves Nemotron-Nano and DeepSeek-V3.1, so the direct-prediction test has 6 judges.
