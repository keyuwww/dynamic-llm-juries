Detecting Best-Model Errors with Order Flips and Confidence

ABWhen the best judge is wrong in both answer orders, can it, or the other judges, warn us? We compare three signals (stated confidence, order flips, disagreement) from two sources: the best judge itself and the weaker judges.

Two questions we explore
  1. Q1Do the best judge's own signals, its confidence and its order flips, tell us when it is wrong?
  2. Q2Can weaker judges, through their confidence, order flips and disagreement, flag the best judge's errors?

Main findings

The two columns answer Q1 and Q2. Two dimensions: which signal (confidence, order flips, disagreement) and whose signal (the best judge itself or the weaker judges). AUROC for the best judge's errors, all pairs, across the 4 benchmarks (0.5 = chance).

Key takeaways
  • Disagreement among the weaker judges is the best alarm; the best judge's own confidence is close behind.
  • Weaker judges can't pick the right answer as often, but their confidence flags the best judge's errors about as well as its own does.
  • Order flips work only pooled across judges; they mark near-ties, where judges default to the first-shown answer.

Weaker judges as error detectors

Key takeaways
  • Weaker judges' low confidence flags the best judge's errors (AUROC 0.59–0.84), often as well as the best judge's own.
  • Only the weakest judge, Nemotron-Nano, is near chance.
  • One judge's flips alone are weak (0.48–0.73), whatever its accuracy.

How to read this

Each point is one judge on one benchmark. x = the judge's own accuracy. y = AUROC for the best judge's confident errors (0.5 = chance). With stated confidence, 24 of the 28 weaker-judge points are above 0.6, and 6 match or beat the best judge's own confidence (for example Qwen3.5-4B on JudgeBench, 0.84). Nemotron-Nano is the exception at 0.54–0.62. Controlling for the benchmark (permuting accuracy within each benchmark, 50,000 permutations): more accurate judges give better confidence warnings, within-benchmark r = +0.86 (p < 0.001), but this rests mostly on Nemotron-Nano; without it r = +0.46 (p = 0.04). A single judge's flips are a weak flag (0.48–0.73) with no link to its accuracy (r = +0.28, p = 0.24; without Nemotron-Nano −0.05, p = 0.85), consistent with flips marking hard items rather than judge skill; they only help when pooled across judges.

Which single signal flags errors best

Key takeaways
  • Strongest: other judges' disagreement (0.76–0.85) and the best judge's own confidence (0.71–0.84).
  • The best judge's own flip catches 32–57% of its errors with no false alarms.
  • Other judges' flips alone are the weakest signal, but still clearly better than random on every benchmark (AUROC 0.67–0.78, p ≤ 0.001).

Caveats
  • Asking for confidence changed 12–20% of verdicts. Confidence comes from the new run; errors are scored on the stored run.
  • The full run used one sample per verdict. In the 3-sample pilot, only 46–80% of a judge's flips repeated.
  • JudgeBench parsed 92.6%: the 4,096-token cap cut some reasoning.
  • The best judge was chosen on the full data. Few confident errors per benchmark (17–79) means wide intervals, especially at high flip thresholds.
  • Many errors no judge flags look like wrong gold labels (AlpacaFarm 3–1 votes, MT-Bench human). On unanimous AlpacaFarm pairs, other judges' flips reach AUROC 0.76 vs 0.61 on 3–1 pairs.
