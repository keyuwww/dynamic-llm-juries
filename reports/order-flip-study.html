Detecting Best-Model Errors with Order Flips and Confidence

ABWhen the best judge is wrong in both answer orders, can it, or the other judges, warn us? Two separate signals do: the judge's own stated confidence, and other judges changing their pick when the order is swapped.

Main findings

  1. 1
    Weaker judges can't reliably pick the right answer, but their uncertainty still marks the items that are hard to judge, including items where the strongest judge is confidently wrong. Their low stated confidence flags the best judge's confident errors at AUROC 0.59–0.84 for every judge except Nemotron-Nano, and on 6 of 28 judge–benchmark pairs it matches or beats the best judge's own confidence.
  2. 2
    The best judge's stated confidence flags its confident errors (AUROC 0.65–0.84). Answers rated 95–100 are wrong 3–10% of the time; answers rated below 80 are wrong 23–31%.
  3. 3
    The more other judges flip, the likelier the best judge is wrong. Error rates climb from 1–10% when nobody flips to 30–40% when 4 or more flip, but fewer than 14% of pairs reach 4 flips.
  4. 4
    Combining the two helps on 3 of 4 benchmarks (AUROC 0.70–0.86).
  5. 5
    Flips mark near-ties, and ties default to the first-shown answer. Several "why" hypotheses failed: flips don't track whether the judge could solve the question and can't be predicted from the text.

Caveats

  • Asking for confidence changed 12–20% of verdicts. Confidence comes from the new run; errors are scored on the stored run.
  • The full run used one sample per verdict. In the 3-sample pilot, only 46–80% of a judge's flips repeated.
  • JudgeBench parsed 92.6%: the 4,096-token cap cut some reasoning.
  • The best judge was chosen on the full data. Few confident errors per benchmark (17–79) means wide intervals, especially at high flip thresholds.
  • Many errors no judge flags look like wrong gold labels (AlpacaFarm 3–1 votes, MT-Bench human). On unanimous AlpacaFarm pairs, other judges' flips reach AUROC 0.76 vs 0.61 on 3–1 pairs.
