81% vs 62%

After a judge approves a wrong answer it believes, its two re-runs approve it again 81% [74, 87] of the time; 62% [58, 66] for answers it never reaches.

About 1 minute. New this week. Juries of diverse models are the standard proposal for reducing one judge’s biases, so I compared majorities of three verdicts at the same budget: three Qwens, one judge sampled three times, and trios from three different families. The items are the 1,369 original items with a re-checked label and a verdict from every judge. The three trios built from the stronger judges do beat three Qwens, by 2 to 2.7 points of balanced accuracy (the average of all 17 trios barely does), but they are not detectably better than the strongest Qwen voting with itself, 86.9%. So the gain mainly reflects using stronger judges, not mixing families. On the 34 wrong answers the Qwens believe, those trios approve 41–47% against 53% for three Qwens; all three intervals include zero, so this is suggestive at most. And re-running a judge does not wash these errors out: after a judge approves a wrong answer it believes, its two re-runs approve it again 81% of the time, against 62% for wrong answers it never reaches. Re-runs were at temperature 0.7 for six judges (Nemotron-3-Nano is no longer served) and cost $39.
