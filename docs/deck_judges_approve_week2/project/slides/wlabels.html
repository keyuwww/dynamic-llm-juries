THE EFFECT SURVIVES

Qwens’ gap: +56 with labels as released → +43 audited → +35 where both auditors agree.

About 1 minute 15. A wrong answer a judge reaches seven times out of seven is either a confident error or a wrong answer key. So every answer labelled wrong was re-checked: by GLM-5.3, told the key might be wrong and to work the problem itself; again by Kimi-K3.1-Pro from another family (96% agreement); and by Claude Opus 5.5 on a sample. The disputed share climbs with belief: 3% of the answers no Qwen reaches, 58% of those some Qwen reaches seven times out of seven. The new MMLU-Pro data shows the same climb, 9% to 51%, and on math every 7/7 response sat on a disputed key. Where they come from: on BFF-Bench, mistakes in the expert reference. For example, the question gives a fair value of $386,000, the reference uses $368,000, and four correct answers were marked wrong; I checked that one in the text myself. Three more disputed answers come from the same problem’s follow-up question, where the auditor finds other errors in the reference. On JudgeBench, the label is MMLU-Pro’s key, and the disputes are mostly questions where a second option is also defensible. The effect survives removing them: the Qwens’ gap is +56 as released, +43 audited, +35 where both auditors agree. Caveat: the auditors are models and could share the judges’ weakness; human-check packages are ready but nobody has read them yet. Practical point: when you score a judge against a benchmark, its most confident “errors” are where the key is most often disputed.
