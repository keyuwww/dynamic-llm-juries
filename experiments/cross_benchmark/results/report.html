LLM Judge Analytics — Across Benchmarks

BFF-Bench’s qualitative and judge-level analysis repeated on nine more benchmarks, plus a test of threshold juries against top-k.

Do the BFF-Bench findings hold on other benchmarks?

The same 8 Tinker judges, the same verdict rules and the same analyses, run on every benchmark the team has used or been pointed to. Each tab has the full breakdown; this page compares them.

BFF-Bench’s main finding holds almost everywhere: judges fail on the same items, so juries rarely beat the best single judge. A threshold jury only helps where judge reliability can be predicted before the call, which happened on one benchmark (RewardBench 2 Safety).

  • Errors are shared on every benchmark. Mean pairwise error correlation between the 8 judges is 0.30–0.48, and items that every judge gets right are 2–6× more common than if errors were independent (BFF: 37% vs 8%; AlpacaFarm: 33% vs 6%).
  • The oracle is far above every rule. “Any judge right” sits at 94–99% on every benchmark, while no combination of verdicts gains more than about 5 points over the best single judge. The headroom exists, but nothing observable before the call finds it.
  • Juries help on three benchmarks out of nine. (C)MT-Bench (+4 to +5 points, every jury rule), AlpacaFarm (majority vote, +2.7) and RewardBench 2 Safety (threshold juries, +1.8 to +2.0). On BFF, BFF pairwise, BBEH, JudgeBench, RewardBench 2 (other) and MT-Bench human the best single judge is as good or better; majority vote is significantly worse on BBEH (−3.2) and JudgeBench (−3.9).
  • The best judge changes with the benchmark. gpt-oss-120b on BFF, BFF pairwise, BBEH, RewardBench 2 and MT-Bench human; gpt-oss-20b on Safety; Qwen3.6-35B on JudgeBench; Qwen3.5-9B on (C)MT-Bench; DeepSeek-V3.1 on AlpacaFarm. Choosing the judge per benchmark matters more than combining judges per item.
  • Human label agreement matters everywhere it can be measured. On every benchmark with several human votes, judges agree much less with humans on split-vote items (mean κ: BFF 0.54 → 0.36, (C)MT-Bench 0.72 → 0.56, AlpacaFarm 0.57 → 0.29, MT-Bench human 0.75 → 0.51). Reporting only unanimous items inflates every judge.
  • Why judges fail differs by benchmark. Missed errors dominate on BFF, BFF pairwise, Safety and BBEH; on JudgeBench the best judge mostly solves the problem itself and gets it wrong; on AlpacaFarm it prefers style over substance; on RewardBench 2 it is too strict on minor deviations. See the Question analysis tab.
  • No sign of contamination. The question-completion probe finds no benchmark where the strongest judge reproduces questions verbatim more than a small control model does.

Threshold vs top-k. Requiring p ≥ τ only pays off when the reliability predictor can actually separate good from bad judge–item pairs. On Safety (predictor AUROC 0.72) keeping only top-3 judges with p ≥ 0.8 beats both top-3 and the best single judge (+2.0 points, 2.3 judges per item). Everywhere else the predictor is near chance (AUROC 0.49–0.63), so p mostly reflects each judge’s overall accuracy: a high τ then cuts the jury to one or two judges picked on noise, and it loses accuracy (AlpacaFarm −2.3, JudgeBench −2.3, BFF pairwise −5.0). Dropping low-p judges is the right idea, but it needs a better per-item reliability signal than question text, category and length; candidates are the judge’s own confidence (5-sample vote share) or agreement between cheap judges after the call.

Caveats

  • Gold labels differ in kind: expert votes (BFF, (C)MT-Bench, MT-Bench human), crowd votes (AlpacaFarm), human chosen / rejected (Safety), verified answers (JudgeBench, BBEH, RewardBench 2 Math) and labels by construction (RewardBench 2 Focus). RewardBench 2 other was subsampled to 60 prompts per subset, MT-Bench human to 400 groups, BBEH to 2 answers per question.
  • Every judge gives one sample per verdict (T = 0.7, no reasoning mode where it can be switched off), so there is no per-item judge confidence. Unparseable verdicts count as the negative verdict; on BBEH several judges run out of tokens while solving the puzzle (gpt-oss-20b parses 75%). Parse rates are in each tab.
  • Aspect codes, error categories and content codes are LLM-authored (gpt-oss-120b and DeepSeek-V3.1), not expert labels; read them as leads.
  • DeepSeek-V3.1 timed out on Tinker for part of the run and was filled in afterwards.
  • TriviaQA (“Judging the Judges”) was not run: its human labels were never released.
  • llm-judge-dpo-5k has only 15 distinct items; its tab is a sanity check, not a measurement.

What makes an item hard to judge, and which judges are good at what

Every judged item across nine benchmarks, plus content coding of 50 judge-hard and 50 judge-easy items per benchmark and a close reading of about 50 items. Difficulty = share of the 8 judges that disagree with the gold label. Full write-up: docs/qualitative_question_analysis.md.

Judges are reliable when they can redo the work and unreliable when grading needs a fact, a rule or a standard they don’t share with the annotators. Which judge is better depends mostly on fixed traits — whether it reasons and how lenient it is — not on the topic of the question.

  • Human disagreement is the most consistent difficulty factor. On every multi-vote benchmark, items the annotators split on are 10–14 points harder (BFF 22% → 32% judges wrong, (C)MT-Bench 9% → 22%, AlpacaFarm 22% → 36%, MT-Bench human 17% → 31%), and some hard items simply have wrong labels.
  • Many “errors” are a different grading standard, not weak reasoning. BFF annotators grade intermediate steps; judges grade the final answer. RewardBench 2 Focus labels reward staying on topic; judges nitpick facts, so good answers are the hard ones there (36% vs 19%). Safety labels mean “appropriate” (challenge false premises, decline impossible tasks); judges read “harmless”, so benign-looking failures are hardest (harm-risk 0: 36% wrong; clearly harmful requests: 11%).
  • Wrong answers that look right are the main trap. In single grading, bad answers are harder than good ones on BFF, (C)MT-Bench, Safety and BBEH, because most judges lean to “correct”.
  • Knowledge beats computation as a difficulty driver. Hard items are more often factual-knowledge or rule questions (33% and 9% of hard items vs 24% and 3% of easy ones); calculations are easier (25% vs 33%). On JudgeBench, MMLU-Pro health / psychology / biology / law are 29–40% wrong vs 12–15% for math, logic and code. Judges can recompute; they can’t check a fact they don’t know, so they grade against their own belief.
  • Reasoning is the judge trait that matters most. The two always-reasoning gpt-oss judges agree with the gold label more than the six thinking-off judges on all 9 benchmarks, significantly on 7 (+3.2 to +10.6 points; largest on BBEH, BFF pairwise and RewardBench 2), and the gap grows on wrong answers: they recompute instead of trusting the answer’s arithmetic. They are also stricter than BFF’s annotators on slips in illustrative examples.
  • Leniency explains much of the rest. False-positive rates range from 0.09 to 0.55 across judges and benchmarks. DeepSeek-V3.1 and Nemotron-Super-120B are the most lenient (DeepSeek accepts 55% of wrong BBEH answers and 36% of inappropriate Safety responses); Qwen3.6-35B is the strictest on BFF. On benchmarks where most answers are bad (Safety, BBEH) leniency costs the most.
  • Topic specialties are small. Outside a few cases (gpt-oss-20b +24 on Safety “unsupported requests”, Qwen3.6-35B +19 on BFF pairwise taxation, Nemotron-Nano-30B collapsing on code and banking), a judge’s relative agreement moves by less than ±5 points across categories and demand levels — which is why per-question judge selection finds little room.

What this suggests for dynamic juries

  1. Route on the kind of verification an item needs: answers that can be recomputed or traced go to a reasoning judge; items resting on facts, rules or conventions are where every judge is weak — escalate them or give the judge a reference.
  2. Fix the grading standard before choosing the judge: a rubric that matches the annotation guidelines (intermediate steps, “appropriate” vs “harmless”, on-topic vs fully accurate) targets the largest share of disagreement.
  3. Correct each judge’s leniency (per-benchmark threshold, or Dawid–Skene style separate weights for its “correct” and “incorrect” verdicts) before combining judges.
  4. Report split-vote items separately and do not count a judge as wrong where the humans themselves disagree.
