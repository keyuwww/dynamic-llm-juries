When a Judge Is Confidently Wrong

Each judge's verdicts are split by its own confidence (low, intermediate, high) and by outcome (right, wrong, flip). We compare confident errors with unsure errors and with right answers at the same confidence, using other judges, the items, the reasoning text and the model's hidden states.

Three questions
  1. Q1Are confident errors shared with other judges more than unsure errors?
  2. Q2What kind of item and mistake is a confident error?
  3. Q3Does the judge know it is wrong: in its token probability, its reasoning, or its hidden states?
Experiment setup
8 judges4 pairwise benchmarks1,614 pairs, both orders120 verdicts coded blind0 open models probed

Outcome per judge and pair: right in both answer orders, wrong in both, or flip. Confidence level: the judge's own lower, middle and upper third of stated confidence (0–100) within each benchmark, so "high" means relatively sure for that judge. Qwen states 95–100 on most items while gpt-oss-120b rarely goes above 95, so fixed cut-offs would not compare judges fairly. Results are checked with calibrated-risk levels and fixed 70 / 90 cuts.

    Confident errors are shared by more judges

    • The gradient holds under all three ways of cutting confidence.

    For every verdict that is wrong in both orders: how many of the other 7 judges are also wrong in both orders on that pair. Bars: 95% CI, bootstrap over questions. The null assumes judges err independently at their own error rates. Only 13–15% of errors at any level are unique to one judge.

    Confident errors sit on contested labels and unsolved problems

    Key takeaways
    • The higher the confidence, the more a wrong verdict sits on items where human annotators disagreed.
    • On BFF, confident errors happen where the judge couldn't solve the problem itself.
    • Unsure errors follow surface cues instead: similar answers, the same final number.

Right of zero: wrong verdicts have more of it. Human split only exists for AlpacaFarm and MT-Bench human; "judge solved it" only for BFF.

    About two thirds of confident errors are label problems or near ties

    Key takeaways
    • High-confidence errors: have a questionable gold label or are a near tie.
    • Unsure errors are mostly near ties. High-confidence flips are the judge's own slips.
    gold label questionablenear tiejudge's own mistake
    How to read this

    120 verdicts, 20 per cell, 5 per benchmark, coded blind to confidence and flips by four Claude coders with a fixed codebook. "Judge's own mistake" = missed factual error, arithmetic or logic slip, misread question, or preferred length or style. HF counts only flip verdicts that were wrong in the shown order (n = 8). A human check of 30 items is pending.

    A confident error reads like a confident right answer

    Key takeaways
    • Blind readers can't tell high-confidence wrong from high-confidence right by tone (AUROC 0.49).
    • Unsure verdicts hedge and call the answers close, whether right or wrong.
    reads highly confidenthedges or self-correctssays the answers are close

    The judge shows little doubt; other judges show more

    Key takeaways
    • Within a confidence level, the judge's own token probability barely separates wrong from right (≈0.55).
    • Other judges' doubt works best on confident errors (0.72 at high vs 0.59 at low).

    About this tab

    Open judges were run on Modal with a forced verdict: the same pairwise prompt, answered immediately with "VERDICT:" and no reasoning, in both orders. One forward pass gives the model's own P(A) vs P(B) (its verdict and confidence) and its hidden states at three points: after the question, after both answers, and at the verdict. Linear probes (PCA 64 + logistic regression, nested cross-validation by question) test whether hidden states separate wrong from right at the same confidence level.

            Caveats
            • One sample per verdict. Study A levels come from the confidence run, which changed 12–20% of verdicts relative to the stored run.
            • Study B uses a forced verdict with no reasoning, so its states differ from the reasoning judges in Study A; the "reasoned verdict" panel links the two.
            • Study B: gpt-oss-120b ran in native MXFP4 weights on one H100, gpt-oss-20b dequantized to bf16; Qwen3.6-35B and Nemotron were not probed. About 35–67 high-confidence errors per model, so high-level probe AUROCs carry intervals of about ±0.08.
            • Coding is by LLM coders, one per record, 20 per cell; Janys's 30-item agreement check is pending.
            • Human split exists only for AlpacaFarm and MT-Bench human; solve labels only for BFF.
            • Judges and pairs overlap across analyses, so points are not independent; intervals bootstrap over questions.
