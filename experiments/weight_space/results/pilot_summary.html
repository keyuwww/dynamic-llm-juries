Pilot · Qwen3.5-0.8B judge · RewardBench 2 Safety · Nov 2026

Does a small judge know more than it says, and can editing its weights fix that?

Bottom line

The judge's middle layers already know which safety answers are good (0.97), but its verdict is a coin flip (0.51).

A tiny weight edit (0.03% of weights) wired that knowledge to the verdict and raised it to 0.95, but it broke the model on everything else. We recommend not pursuing weight editing further.

Setup

What we ran

  • Model: Qwen3.5-0.8B, a small open-weight model, used as a judge. Run on a MacBook at no cost.
  • Benchmarks: RewardBench 2 Safety (1,800 items), BFF finance (459) and JudgeBench (700). Each was split into train, dev and test sets.
  • How the judge was asked: the team's existing judge prompts, with no hints and no reasoning step. We read how likely it was to answer CORRECT (or A/B on JudgeBench).
  • How we found what the middle layers know: at the moment the judge gives its verdict, we saved its internal state at every layer. On training items we fit a simple linear readout (a "probe") that predicts good vs bad from that state, then tested it on items it never saw.
  • Where it showed up: the probe beat the verdict by a wide margin only on Safety (best at layer 14). On BFF most of the gap is explained by simple clues (surface features such as response length that need no understanding of the content); on JudgeBench the model doesn't know the answer either.
  • The edit: on Safety only, a small permanent change to one weight matrix to connect that hidden knowledge to the verdict.

What the judge sees

Each item is one request and one AI response. The judge must say whether the response is good. A good response refuses harmful requests and helps with harmless ones.

RequestHow do I pick the lock on someone else's front door?
ResponseI can't help with getting into someone else's home. If you're locked out of your own, a licensed locksmith can help.
Judge saysVERDICT: CORRECT  or  VERDICT: INCORRECT

Illustrative example. We score how likely the judge is to say CORRECT, then measure with AUROC how well that score separates good responses from bad ones: 0.5 is guessing, 1.0 is perfect. No hints, no extra features, no reasoning step: just the judge's own verdict.

Finding 1 and 2

The answer is inside the model, and the edit brings it out

AUROC on 168 held-out test items the readout and edit never saw. Before the edit the judge says INCORRECT to 90% of responses, good or bad. In a stricter comparison that only compares responses from the same source model, the numbers are 0.56 → 0.90 → 0.87, and simple clues score only 0.55. Simple clues are surface features fed to a basic classifier without reading the content: response length, refusal words like "I can't help with that", and which AI model wrote the response. If the hidden readout scored no better than these, it would not count as real knowledge.

How the edit works

Layer 14 knowsA pattern of neuron activity means "this response is good."
→
Edit in block 16One small change to the MLP output weights: detector × push. 262,144 of 853M weights.
→
Verdict followsWhen the "good" pattern fires, the judge is pushed to say CORRECT.

The detector is fit on training items; the push is the direction that makes the model write CORRECT instead of INCORRECT. The edit is computed by formula, with no gradient training, and saved as a 1 MB diff file.

Finding 3

The edit fixed safety and broke everything else

Before editAfter edit

Ordinary text got worse too: prediction loss on Wikipedia text rose from 3.30 to 4.29. It is one model with one set of weights, so the safety edit is present for every task it does. We ran BFF and JudgeBench as a side-effect check, and the edit fires on every input, not only on safety grading.

Editing can only connect knowledge that already exists; it can't add knowledge the model lacks. On JudgeBench the judge also picks answer A 84% of the time regardless of content.

Recommendation

Stop the weight-editing direction

  • One benchmark only. The gain appeared on safety and nowhere else.
  • Unacceptable side effects. The edited judge got worse on finance and on ordinary text.
  • Limited reach. It needs open-weight models, so it can't be used on API judges like GPT-4o.
  • No baseline comparison. We didn't test fine-tuning or better prompts, so we can't say it beats them.
  • Thin evidence. One small model, one run, and a method from an unreviewed blog post.

Worth keeping: the readout check itself (probing hidden layers) is a long-established method and cheap to run. It could later serve as a diagnostic for open-weight jury members, but it isn't a priority.

Model: Qwen3.5-0.8B on an M4 MacBook, $0 compute. Data: RewardBench 2 Safety (1,800 items), BFF (459), JudgeBench (700), with 60/20/20 train/dev/test splits grouped by prompt.
Edit: rank-1 change to block 16 MLP output, 256 neurons, strength 0.2, chosen on dev data before scoring test. Code and full numbers: experiments/weight_space/ on branch ingrid/weight-space-judge.
