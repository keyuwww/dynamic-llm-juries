Drill-down · Qwen3.5-0.8B judge · RewardBench 2 Safety and BFF

Where the judge's hidden safety knowledge comes from, and whether editing it is significant

Bottom line

The safety knowledge is built by a small number of parts pushing in opposite directions, mostly attention heads in the two standard-attention layers 8 and 16, plus the MLPs in layers 8, 9 and 14. 36 of 240 parts beat the random-label control.

The 256 neurons we edited before were no better than a random pick. On BFF the map is weak and noisy, with only one significant part.

The weight edit is significant: it beats shuffled labels, random directions and random changes 19 of 19 times. But any 256 neurons work equally well, and editing later layers (16, 20) gives the same gain with far less damage.

Setup

What we ran

  • Model and data: Qwen3.5-0.8B judge, all 900 RewardBench 2 Safety items with the team's safety prompt, and all 459 BFF items with the team's finance prompt. Forward passes ran on a Modal GPU.
  • What we recorded: at the moment the judge gives its verdict, the output of every attention head and every MLP in all 24 layers. Each layer has either 16 heads (linear attention) or 8 heads (standard attention, in layers 4, 8, 12, 16), plus one MLP with 3,584 neurons.
  • The knowledge readout: the same simple probe as before, read at the layer where it works best (layer 16 for Safety, 0.95 AUROC, while the verdict is 0.53).
  • Splitting it up: the model's internal state is exactly the sum of what every head and MLP adds (checked: the parts add up within 1%). So the probe's score splits into one share per part.
  • Random control: we shuffled the good/bad labels 500 times within each request. A part counts as significant (★, dark outline) only if it beats the largest push any part gets from shuffled labels.
Push = how much a part moves the model's internal state toward "this response is good" for good responses, and toward "bad" for bad ones. Shown as a share of the total.
Positive (blue) = helps build the right answer. Negative (orange) = pushes toward the wrong answer and is cancelled out by other parts. All shares add up to 100%.

Part 1 summary

What the map tells us

  • Localized, but not to one spot: the safety knowledge comes from about a dozen parts with large pushes, concentrated in the standard-attention layers 8 and 16 and nearby MLPs.
  • Push and pull: several large parts push the wrong way and are cancelled by others. A coarse edit that touches both sides is likely to cause side effects.
  • The earlier edit was untargeted: its 256 neurons were a near-random pick, which explains why it was blunt.
Part 2

Was the weight edit significant, or would a random edit do?

Setup

What we ran

  • The real edit: the same recipe as the earlier experiment, rebuilt under the team's safety prompt. In layer 17's MLP, take the 256 neurons that track the hidden knowledge most closely, learn a detector from them, and add a push toward "say CORRECT" scaled by the detector (0.03% of the weights).
  • Random copies: 19 copies each, changing one ingredient at a time for a random version (table below), plus the same recipe in layers 9, 13, 16 and 20.
  • Fair size: every edit was scaled to change the model's internal state by the same amount, so only the ingredient differs.
  • No peeking: everything was fit on training items and scored on the 168 held-out Safety test items, the 74 BFF test items and 20 Wikipedia passages. 84 edits ran on 10 Modal GPUs in parallel.

Best is top left: a big Safety gain with little damage. Three things stand out:

  • Random changes are harmless (text loss +0.04), even though they're the same size. The damage isn't from changing weights; it's from what the edit writes.
  • Shuffled-label edits are the most harmful (text loss +5.1 on average) and gain nothing. A detector without real knowledge fires on everything.
  • Later layers are gentler. Layers 16 and 20 match the real edit's Safety gain (0.97, 0.95) with about half the text damage (+1.2 vs +2.1) and almost no finance damage (BFF −0.03, −0.01 vs −0.10). Layer 9 is the worst on both.
What it all means

The edit is real, but it's a rewiring, not a precise fix

  • The weight edit is significant. It beats shuffled labels, random directions and random changes 19 out of 19 times. The gain comes from the judge's real hidden knowledge being routed to its verdict.
  • Which neurons doesn't matter. Random neurons in the same layer do just as well. This matches Part 1: the knowledge is readable almost everywhere after layer 6, so any reasonable set of neurons can serve as the detector.
  • Where matters for side effects. Editing later (layers 16 or 20) keeps the Safety gain and cuts the damage, especially to finance grading. Our original layer 17 choice was optimized for Safety only and was not the best trade-off.
  • The remaining damage has one cause: the edit is always on, so the detector also fires on finance questions and normal text. Making it fire only on safety grading, for example by adding "do nothing here" examples when fitting the detector, is the most direct next fix.
  • Connection to Part 1: the parts that build the knowledge (layers 8 and 16 heads) are where a precise intervention would target. Switching them off to test that they cause it is the next step.
