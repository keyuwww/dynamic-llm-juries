Why does a detailed rubric hurt gpt-oss‑20b?

A logit-lens and causal-activation-patching investigation on BFF-Bench, following the method of Wang et al. 2025, “When Truth Is Overridden: Internal Origins of Sycophancy”.

Summary. A detailed rubric raises most judges' agreement with human labels, but lowers gpt-oss‑20b's specifically. Confirmed with BFF-Bench's real human-annotator rubric: κ drops 0.667→0.617; removing one rule — steps must match the reference, not just the answer — fully restores it (0.666). gpt-oss‑120b is unaffected.

Under a reasoning-free forced-choice probe, gpt-oss‑20b's effect nearly vanishes (1 of 459 items flip). Main results below use a stricter researcher-written rubric instead, which shows a late, switch-like computation for gpt-oss‑20b (layer ≈22/24, 81% causal suppression). A secondary tab keeps the real-rubric results — strong for 4 of 5 other judges, including the study's strongest effect: nemotron3-nano-30b's 99%/95% reversible pivot.

Update (§5): a separate question — does a judge's own interior layer already agree with human labels better than its final answer does? — tested with held-out validation across 7 models × 3 benchmarks (BFF-Bench, JudgeBench, RewardBench2-Safety). gpt-oss-120b and Qwen3.5-9B are stable, benchmark-independent in opposite directions (always / never shows the effect); everyone else is domain-dependent. A real, human-authored rubric found in CoCoNot's own appendix sharpens this effect rather than just preserving it, on an independent benchmark from the one §1–4 are built on.

Update (§7): a third strict rubric-vs-standard pair — HealthBench, OpenAI's own physician meta-eval, excluding RewardBench2-Safety's borrowed rubric — plus 200-split bootstrap CIs and permutation p-values (every row p<1e−5), three more models confirming the effect is gpt-oss-120b-specific, a head-level dissection of BiGGen's rubric condition (naive attribution's loudest layer, 31, is a causally-empty red herring; the true 100%-causal layers, 13–15, have no single responsible head either — a genuinely diffuse mechanism), and, across all eight DLA/self-patch pairs run, a falsifiable predictor of when direct logit attribution mislocalizes the cause: not MLP-dominance (our first guess, broken by an eighth data point), but how early the true best layer sits (R²=0.87 between benchmarks, confirmed at the per-item level within benchmarks too). An independent study across 41 judge models (arXiv:2609.32407) finds the identical late-attribution/early-cause pattern, which is evidence this is a property of the attribution method itself, not a quirk of our benchmarks or model.

1. Two rubrics

A rubric was built from scratch to probe this effect, then turned out to differ from the real one behind BFF-Bench's labels — Appendix A.4 of Setlur et al., “No Free Labels”, shown below. §2 uses the real rubric; §4's main results use ours instead (§3 explains why).

Verbatim: Appendix A.4, “Annotator Guidelines,” No Free Labels
  1. If the model's final answer or recommendation disagrees with the reference, it should be marked “Incorrect”.
  2. If the model agrees with the reference but its intermediate steps disagree, it should also be marked “Incorrect”.
  3. If the model agrees with the reference, all of the intermediate steps agree, and it provides the additional information that is not obviously wrong based on the reference, it should be marked “Correct”.
  4. If the model agrees with the reference, all of the intermediate steps agree, but the answer is slightly off due to rounding or performing approximate calculations, it should be marked as “Correct”.
  5. If the model agrees with the reference, all of the intermediate steps agree, but it is lacking some amount of information given within the reference, you should make a judgment call as to whether the missing information is crucial to the answer. If you are unsure, lean towards “Correct” or mark it “Unsure”.

Source: arXiv:2503.05061v4, Appendix A.4 — written for human annotators, reused here verbatim as the LLM judge's grading instructions.

3. Why the main results use our rubric

Every interp number needs a forced-choice probe: no reasoning, an immediate CORRECT/INCORRECT read off the logits. That's a big handicap vs. how judges normally work. Accuracy with reasoning vs. without it shows how much each one depends on it:

The real rubric's one strict rule needs reasoning to evaluate — clean signal for 4 of 5 judges, silent for gpt-oss‑20b. Our rubric stacks several bluntly-worded conditions instead, biasing an immediate token read directly, without reasoning: more sensitive, not more faithful.

4. Mechanistic results

Our rubric (main tab) works in every model. The real Appendix A.4 rubric (secondary tab) works for 4 of 5 judges, not gpt-oss‑20b, but includes this study's strongest causal effect where it does.

Layer-wise decision tracking

At every layer, the hidden state is read through the model's own final norm and unembedding (the logit lens) to see which verdict the model would commit to if it stopped there. We track P(CORRECT) = pCORRECT / (pCORRECT + pINCORRECT), a clean 0-1 probability (the same quantity used internally for the KL divergence below), restricted to items whose verdict actually flips between prompts — averaging in items that never flip would wash out the signal.

Takeaway 2 gpt-oss-20b is one-sided (24 stricter, ~0 lenient). gpt-oss-120b flips both ways almost equally (96 vs. 95) — more perturbed at the token level, but the directions cancel out, matching its small net κ change in §2.

Causal activation patching

Correlation is not causation. For every flip item, we swap the hidden state at its critical layer (3-layer window, 1 layer for the smaller Qwen patch) between conditions and re-run the model.

5. Does an earlier layer already know more than the final answer?

Every figure so far compares one layer's reading against another prompt's reading at that same layer. This section asks a different question instead: compare each layer's own implied verdict directly against the human gold label, not against the other condition. If some interior layer already agrees with humans better than the model's own final output does, that final output is discarding signal, not adding it.

Takeaway For most judges tested, the model's own final verdict is not the point of peak agreement with humans — an earlier layer already "knows" more, and whatever happens between that layer and the output token makes the judge's stated answer less reliable, not more. gpt-oss-120b is the extreme case: its final answer is actively anti-informative under the rubric prompt, while a single interior layer alone would make a moderately-agreeing judge. This is model-specific, not universal — Qwen3.5-9B's final layer is genuinely the best one it has. (The numbers above are full-sample; see the held-out validation directly below, which revises several of them.)

Is this real, or just picking the best of many noisy layers?

Comparing a single final-token prediction against the best of 24–88 layers is a multiple-comparisons problem — with enough layers, one will beat the final answer by chance alone. To correct for this, each judge/benchmark pair below was tested with 20 random 50/50 splits: pick the best layer on one half, then score that specific layer against the final answer on the other, held-out half. A real effect should survive this; a selection artifact should not.

Validated finding Correcting for selection bias changes the picture in important ways. gpt-oss-120b is the one model where an interior layer robustly beats the final answer on every benchmark tested (19–20 of 20 splits each time) — this looks like a genuine property of the model, not of any one task. Qwen3.5-9B is the mirror image: its final layer is the best layer on every benchmark, with zero of 20 splits ever favoring an earlier one — also a stable, benchmark-independent property, just in the opposite direction. Everyone else is domain-dependent: Qwen3.5-4B shows the single largest effect in the whole study on RewardBench2-Safety (κ +0.14→+0.78) but no effect at all on JudgeBench; the Nemotron-3 family shows a strong, robust effect on BFF-Bench and RewardBench2-Safety at 30B but it shrinks to near-noise at 120B on every benchmark (BFF +0.25→+0.06, RB2-Safety +0.19→+0.00) — the larger model's final answer is already near the agreement ceiling (κ≈+0.91 on RB2-Safety), leaving an interior layer little room to add anything. gpt-oss-20b shows essentially nothing anywhere — its final answer is a constant, content-blind prediction on both RewardBench2-Safety (always "CORRECT") and JudgeBench (always "A"), and on RewardBench2-Safety that constancy holds at every layer, not just the output. This is a harsher version of the reasoning-dependency already noted in §6: the forced single-token probe may simply be the wrong instrument for this particular model, more so than for any other judge tested. JudgeBench is the one benchmark where the effect is weak or absent for every model except gpt-oss-120b, hinting that pairwise comparison may just need more layers' worth of integration than a binary correctness call does.

When the effect is present, where in the network does it live? For gpt-oss-120b (36 layers), the best layer sits in the same late band on all three benchmarks — 31/37 (84%) on BFF, 35/37 (95%) on JudgeBench, 28/37 (76%) on RB2-Safety — deep, but never the literal final layer. Qwen3.5-4B shows the same clustering (82–91% depth) despite its final-answer accuracy swinging wildly across those same three benchmarks. Qwen3.5-9B's best layer, by contrast, is the final layer (or one before it) every time. The picture this suggests: these models compute a good verdict somewhere in their last ~10–20% of layers, and what happens after that point either preserves it (Qwen3.5-9B), discards it consistently (gpt-oss-120b, Qwen3.5-4B), or has nothing left to discard because the final answer was already near-ceiling (nemotron3-super-120b).

Is it the same layer winning every time?

The gap and "x/20" counts above say the sign of the effect is reliable, but not whether it's the same layer winning each time or a different lucky layer every split. Re-running the 20 held-out splits and tracking which exact layer gets picked on half A answers that directly: if the winner jumps around randomly from split to split, the effect is likely noise even when its sign is consistent; if the same layer (or a tight cluster) wins almost every time, that's independent evidence of a real, located mechanism.

Practical implication for the jury The held-out evaluation above is already the relevant test for deployment: it asks whether reading a fixed layer index (chosen without looking at the test item) beats reading the final token — exactly the question that matters for an early-exit judge. For gpt-oss-120b, Qwen3.5-4B (on BFF and RB2-Safety), and nemotron3-nano-30b (on RB2-Safety), the answer is yes, reliably: stopping forward computation partway through and reading the logit-lens verdict there would both cut inference cost and raise agreement with human labels, not trade one for the other. The catch is that the right layer is model- and domain-specific (compare the per-model columns in Figure 12), so this isn't a universal recipe — it would need calibrating per judge, per task, on a held-out set, before using it in the jury itself.

Does a real, human-authored rubric replicate the BFF-Bench story on a second benchmark?

§1–3 found that adding a detailed, human-authored rubric (Appendix A.4) to BFF-Bench changes which models agree with humans at the final token versus an interior layer. RewardBench2's own Safety appendix doesn't quote a reusable rubric (see below), but it names its source: CoCoNot (Brahman et al., 2024), whose own Appendix A, Table 4 does give a verbatim, human-authored definition of acceptable vs. unacceptable behavior for its "Dangerous or Sensitive Topics" category — the one that matches RB2-Safety's CoCoNot-derived prompts most directly. Dropping that exact text into the judge prompt (replacing the plain "is this appropriate?" framing used elsewhere in this report) and rerunning the held-out validation:

Replicated, with a real rubric, on an independent benchmark gpt-oss-120b's final answer collapses to pure chance under the CoCoNot rubric (κ=+0.003, down from an already-weak no-rubric final answer) while an interior layer still reaches +0.24 — the gap widens from +0.19 to +0.21, and the best layer shifts notably earlier (76%→49% depth), suggesting the rubric engages a different, earlier mechanism rather than just amplifying the same one. Qwen3.5-4B's gap grows from +0.63 to +0.71 — the single largest effect in this entire study (final κ=+0.12, best layer κ=+0.83). Qwen3.5-9B stays flat either way (+0.02 with the rubric vs. −0.00 without) — the one model whose final layer is trustworthy regardless of rubric, task, or benchmark. This is the clearest evidence in the whole report that "a detailed rubric makes some judges' final answer less reliable, not more" is not a BFF-Bench quirk.

6. Scope of these findings

Not yet shown: a mechanism for gpt-oss‑20b's own real-rubric effect — the model that started this investigation. Its κ drop (0.667→0.617) is real, but comes from the full reasoning pipeline; strip reasoning out for the forced-choice probe and the flip set collapses to 1 of 459 items, too few to patch, even though four other judges work fine under the same setup. Fixing this likely needs the model to reason first, then be read at the final token.
Why JudgeBench never gets a rubric condition, and RewardBench2-Safety's is borrowed: BFF-Bench's rubric text in §1 is reused verbatim from a real annotator guideline (Appendix A.4 of its source paper) — we checked whether JudgeBench and RewardBench2 have an equivalent before testing anything on them, including their own appendices (and, for RewardBench2's cited source, that source's own appendix too). JudgeBench's gold labels come from a purely algorithmic pipeline (regex answer-matching, each source dataset's own checker, cross-checked by GPT-4o-mini) with no human-written grading rubric anywhere in the paper — Figure 12's JudgeBench column stays rubric-free. RewardBench2's Safety appendix (G.4) confirms human verification of examples happened and names its rubric's source as CoCoNot's taxonomy, but never reproduces the text itself; the verbatim definition is in CoCoNot's own (Brahman et al., 2024) Appendix A, Table 4, used in Figure 13 above. It's an upstream source, not a confirmed match to RB2's own internal wording (RB2 says it "modified" CoCoNot's taxonomy without saying how), so Figure 12's RewardBench2-Safety column stays plain-prompt and Figure 13 is a separate, labeled replication on three of those five models.

7. A third strict benchmark, formal significance, cross-model generality, and the BiGGen‑rubric anomaly

Everything above used informal held-out estimates (20 splits, no confidence interval). This section adds: (a) a physician-written rubric benchmark with no analogue in CoCoNot/RB2's taxonomy problem, (b) 200-split bootstrap confidence intervals and permutation p-values for every benchmark/condition pair, (c) three more models run on BiGGen and HealthBench to test generality, and (d) a head-level dissection that resolves exactly where BiGGen-rubric's causal effect lives versus where naive attribution falsely points.

7.1 HealthBench: OpenAI's own physician meta-eval

BFF-Bench (§1) and BiGGen Bench (§5) both carry genuine, benchmark-specific human-written rubrics. RewardBench2-Safety's rubric condition (Figure 13) does not — it borrows CoCoNot's upstream taxonomy, not RB2's own annotator text, so it stays out of the strict rubric-vs-standard comparison (see the scope note above). To get a third strict pair, we used OpenAI's HealthBench meta-evaluation release (arXiv:2505.08775, data via openai/simple-evals): 29,511 (conversation, completion, rubric-criterion) triples, each with a rubric criterion written verbatim by a physician and 2+ physician binary judgments on whether that completion meets it. We keep the 77.5% with unanimous physician agreement (22,870 rows) as gold, matching this project's BFF consensus=unanimous policy, and run two conditions: standard (holistic “is this response good?”, no rubric shown) and rubric (the physician's exact criterion shown, instructional wrapper adapted verbatim from OpenAI's own grading template).

Takeaway Every gap in this project, in either direction, is statistically indistinguishable from chance being the explanation only on paper — in practice, all nine rows hit p<1e−5 and tight confidence intervals, with ≥73% of splits landing within ±2 layers of the same winner. HealthBench-standard is the one case with a genuinely negative, significant gap — its own final layer (literally layer 36/36 in 100% of winning splits) is the single best layer, the only benchmark/condition in the whole project where that's true for gpt-oss-120b. The physician rubric flips this into a positive, real (if modest, κ+0.025) effect — a rubric doesn't just change the size of the effect here, it changes its sign.

7.4 Resolving the BiGGen-rubric anomaly: where does the effect actually live?

Direct logit attribution (DLA) — projecting each layer's own attention/MLP write onto the CORRECT/INCORRECT direction in a single ordinary forward pass, no intervention — agrees closely with the true causal rescue layer (found by same-pass activation patching) on some benchmark/conditions but not others. A second correction. Adding the eighth pair, BFF's standard (no-rubric) condition, broke our previous correlate. BFF-standard is only 23% MLP-dominant — mostly attn-dominant, the opposite of the >90%-MLP-dominance signature we'd attributed to the two prior mislocalizing cases — yet it mislocalizes worse than anything else in the project (median +21 layers). Across all eight points, MLP-dominance and attribution error are essentially uncorrelated (Pearson r=0.13, R²=0.02). What actually predicts it is how early the true best layer L is: r(L, median diff)=−0.93, R²=0.87. DLA's own peak-push layer clusters in a narrow, nearly fixed late band regardless of benchmark — 30–36 of 37 in every case we checked, including BFF-standard (70/93 items peak at layer 32) and BiGGen-rubric (64/68 at layer 31) — while the true causal layer tracks L itself (BFF-standard's causal layer clusters at 11–13, right after its L=10; BiGGen-rubric's at 13–15, after its L=4). When L is already late (L≥28 in our data), DLA's generic late-band bias happens to land near the true cause, and attribution looks accurate. When L is early, the same fixed late-band bias overshoots by however far away L is, and attribution mislocalizes badly. This reads as a genuine DLA artifact — raw per-layer writes project more strongly onto the readout direction simply by virtue of being closer to the final unembedding, independent of which layer's computation is actually causally responsible — now pinned to a concrete, falsifiable predictor (L) rather than a mechanism-type guess (MLP-dominance, or, before that, per-instance rubrics) that didn't survive an eighth data point. This is not just an artifact of our own 8 pairs: arXiv:2609.32407 independently runs the same DLA-vs-true-cause comparison across 41 different judge models and finds the identical pattern — DLA correlates with the true causal ranking at only ρ=0.31, with a peak-attribution layer around layer 30 against a peak-cause layer around layer 18 (DLA late, true cause earlier, same direction and comparable size to what we find). That is real, independent, larger-scale support that this is a property of DLA itself, not a quirk of our benchmarks or of gpt-oss-120b.

A sharper, within-benchmark test of the artifact account (using data already on disk, no new Modal runs): the between-benchmark correlation above is consistent with a weaker alternative — that DLA genuinely tracks the true cause, just with a per-benchmark miscalibration. The artifact account makes a stronger, item-level prediction that rules this out: within a single benchmark, individual items' true causal layer (found by self-patch) varies from item to item, but if DLA's peak is really pinned to a fixed network depth rather than reading the cause, that peak should not track each item's own causal layer. It doesn't: across the seven benchmark/conditions with within-benchmark variance in the causal layer, the mean item-level correlation between an item's own DLA-peak layer and its own causal layer is r=0.055 — essentially zero — even where the causal layer itself varies substantially (e.g. BiGGen-rubric: causal-layer variance 13.4 vs. peak-layer variance 0.9; the true cause moves around by up to a dozen layers while DLA's peak barely moves at all). One exception: BFF-rules shows r=0.815, which we don't yet have an explanation for and flag as unresolved rather than quietly excluding. Aside from that case, the fixed-late-band account predicts exactly this pattern and the "DLA tracks the cause, just miscalibrated per-benchmark" alternative does not.

The BiGGen-rubric mechanism, fully dissected Neither location has a single responsible head. Layer 31 is a red herring: it dominates naive attribution (large raw logit push, misread as "the cause") but zero-ablating any one head there recovers almost nothing (1.4%) — it is downstream noise, not the mechanism, exactly the failure mode the literature (arXiv:2609.32407) warns attribution is prone to. Layers 13–15 are causally real (same-pass patching rescues 100% of items there) but also have no dominant head — combined with the whole-sublayer finding that 98.5% of items are "passive forgetting" (zero-ablation alone does not recover the verdict; only restoring the actual earlier hidden state does), this rules out a Halawi-et-al-style "a few heads actively reintroduce the wrong answer" story for BiGGen's rubric condition. The rubric-induced degradation here is genuinely diffuse: distributed across many heads/dimensions at an early-middle layer, invisible to naive per-layer attribution, and only recoverable by literally replaying the earlier computation forward — not deletable, head-level or whole-sublayer, at either the true cause or the layer that merely looks loudest.
What §7 does and doesn't establish: the passive-forgetting / attribution-reliability dissociation (RewardBench2-Safety-standard and HealthBench-rubric are both passive-forgetting yet attribution-accurate, while BiGGen-rubric and RewardBench2-Safety-rubric are both passive-forgetting and attribution-inaccurate) shows these are two independent properties of a benchmark/condition, not two readings of the same fact; BFF-standard (mostly active-override, 75%, and also attribution-inaccurate) confirms the two axes are genuinely orthogonal rather than a coincidence of the first seven points. Separately, our hypothesis for what predicts attribution-inaccuracy has now been revised twice: first "per-instance rubrics" (falsified by RewardBench2-Safety's reused CoCoNot rubric still mislocalizing), then ">90% MLP dominance" (falsified by BFF-standard mislocalizing worst of all at only 23% MLP-dominant). The current best-supported predictor, best-layer depth L (r=−0.93, R²=0.87, n=8 between benchmarks; confirmed at the item level within benchmarks, mean r=0.055 between an item's own causal layer and its own DLA-peak layer), is drawn from only eight benchmark/condition pairs on one model and has not been tested by directly manipulating L itself (e.g. via a model or prompt engineered to move L to a different depth) — it is still an observed correlation across benchmarks that differ in many other ways at once, not a confirmed causal account of DLA's artifact, and a ninth data point could break it exactly as the eighth broke the previous guess.
