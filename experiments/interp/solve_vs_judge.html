Solving isn't judging

Why some models are better judges than their problem-solving ability predicts, what separates them, and what that means for juries and debate. Built from data we already have: BBEH, where every pool model both answers and judges, plus the 42 interior-layer cells.

  • Ranks divergeThe best solver (deepseek-v3.1) is the 4th-best judge. qwen3.6-35b solves 5th and judges 2nd.
  • LeniencyMost of the gap between judges comes from how often they say CORRECT, more than from what they know.
  • Interior layerFor the qwen models, the "knows better than it says" effect is mostly a decision threshold set too leniently. The last layer already ranks answers as well as the winning layer. gpt-oss-120b shows a real ranking gain.
  • AnchoringCommitting to a wrong answer hurts judging more than failing to solve. Judges approve wrong answers that match their own wrong answer more often.
  • ReachJudges accept 77% of correct answers from weaker examinees and 44% from stronger ones. This is the regime debate was designed for.

1. Solving and judging rank the models differently

2. The interior-layer result, revisited

The headline mech-interp finding was that a model's best interior layer agrees with humans more than its own final verdict, robustly in gpt-oss-120b and qwen3.5-4b. Splitting each cell's rescues by direction shows they almost always run one way. Where the final verdict says CORRECT too often, nearly every rescue is a case the model should have rejected.

That raises a question: does the interior layer know more, or is the final verdict using a bad cutoff on information it already has? AUROC answers it, because it measures ranking without any cutoff. If the last layer's P(CORRECT) ranks right answers above wrong ones as well as the winning layer does, the knowledge is already at the output and only the threshold is off.

Changes the headline

For qwen3.5-4b and qwen3.6-35b, the interior-layer win reflects a final verdict that is too lenient. The model ranks answers well at its output; it just says CORRECT too easily. A per-model recalibrated threshold would likely recover most of the gap without reading any interior layer.

gpt-oss-120b and nemotron-nano (BFF standard) gain real ranking signal from the interior layer. gpt-oss-120b's last-layer AUROC sits near 0.5, which may be a readout-position issue caused by its reasoning channel. Check that before citing those cells.

3. Being confidently wrong hurts more than not knowing

No Free Labels found that judges do well mainly on items they can solve. On BBEH we can split "failed to solve" into two cases: the judge committed to a wrong answer, or it ran out of tokens before answering.

After committing to a wrong answer, 7 of 8 judges drop to chance. gpt-oss-120b keeps κ 0.18, the only real case of verifying without solving in this pool. Not finishing hurts much less: qwen3.6-35b still judges at κ 0.26 on items where it ran out of tokens.

The approval column could be inflated by attractive wrong answers that fool everyone. To control for that, I compared judges on the same wrong examinee answer (112 item–answer pairs). Judges who had given that same answer approved it 71% of the time (301 judgments). Judges who had committed to a different answer approved it 56% of the time (414 judgments). The anchoring is real, and on top of that most judges are lenient toward wrong answers in general.

Judges reject wrong answers at about the same rate regardless of who wrote them. What collapses is acceptance of correct answers from stronger models: a judge rejects a correct answer it cannot check. The earlier mech-interp confound points the same way. Interior-layer rescues were about 1.4× more common for weaker examinees (gemma-2-2b, yi-1.5-34b) than for gpt-4o and llama-3.3-70b.

A general frame: four properties of a judge

A single κ hides four separate things. Each one can be measured from outputs and from activations, which turns "why is model X a better judge" into four smaller questions.

Discrimination

Can it rank right answers above wrong ones?

Outputs
AUROC of vote share or verdict logprob
Inside
AUROC per layer from logit lens or probes

Threshold

Where does it draw the line between CORRECT and INCORRECT?

Outputs
Says-CORRECT rate vs true rate
Inside
Shift in P(CORRECT) between the winning and last layer

Independence

Does it check the answer, or compare it to its own?

Outputs
Approval of answers matching its own wrong answer, within item
Inside
Does patching in its own-answer representation flip the verdict?

Reach

How strong is it relative to the examinee?

Outputs
Acceptance of right answers by relative strength
Inside
Rescue rate by examinee strength

On how these differences arise: eight models can't support causal claims about training. Two designs can give testable leads. Size ladders within a family (qwen 4b → 9b → 35b, gpt-oss 20b → 120b) separate scale from recipe. Base vs instruct pairs test whether preference tuning produces the leniency. We skipped that comparison earlier, but it now bears directly on the threshold property.

What this means for juries and debate

  • Recalibrate each juror's threshold before voting. Leniency is a per-model offset that a few labels can fix. A plain majority vote inherits it, and a Dawid–Skene-style weighted jury (B3) partly corrects for it.
  • Be careful with solve-then-judge. Method B in the proposal has each judge answer first. The anchoring result says this helps when the judge solves the item and hurts when the judge is confidently wrong. Use it only for judges the certifier expects to solve the item.
  • Use debate where the examinee is stronger than the judge. Debate is designed for a weak judge facing strong debaters, and that is the slice where acceptance of right answers falls to 44%. A dynamic mechanism can route weaker examinees to one calibrated judge and stronger examinees to debate, a reference answer, or escalation.
  • Pick jurors for complementary profiles, not rank. gpt-oss-20b rejects 80% of wrong answers; qwen3.6-35b accepts 71% of right ones. Together they cover each other's weak side.

Caveats

  • BBEH only for sections 1, 3 and 4; one sample per judgment; "strength" means BBEH solve accuracy.
  • About half of solve attempts are truncated. Section 3 separates truncated from wrong answers, but "failed" is still noisy.
  • The true-correct rate differs by examinee, so the acceptance and rejection columns are cleaner reads than overall accuracy.
