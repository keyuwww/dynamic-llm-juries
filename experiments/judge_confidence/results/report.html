When judges are confidently wrong

We planted one numeric error at a time into finance answers that experts had approved, and compared how three strong LLM judges handled those errors with how they handled the real mistakes in BFF-Bench. The judges catch planted numbers. They confidently miss real method errors, which the expert reference mostly fixes. And when they confidently reject an answer the experts approved, the answer usually does contain an error. Offered an UNSURE option, they never use it. Small open judges hold more in their hidden states than their verdicts say, but they share the big judges' blind spots.

1 · Planted errors

Strong judges catch a single wrong number, even when it is carried through

DeepSeek-V3.1 edited each approved answer in one controlled way, and a script checked that only the intended numbers changed (421 error texts and 275 harmless edits passed). A "consistent" error changes one intermediate number and recomputes everything after it, so the answer agrees with itself and the judge has to redo the arithmetic to notice. The Validation Gap paper found small models catch only 3–45% of those. Our reasoning judges catch 74%–99% (gpt-oss-120b and Qwen3.6).

2 · Real errors

Real mistakes are confidently missed 3–6 times more often, until the reference arrives

We judged all 960 real BFF answers the same way. Against the 203 answers all experts marked wrong, the judges accept many more with full confidence than they did for planted errors. In 8 of them all three judges were fully confident and wrong. Each of those answers has correct arithmetic built on the wrong method or premise. One example is the IFRS impairment question, where three different answer models gave the same $116,550 and every judge agreed. With the expert reference, none of the 203 fool all three judges.

3 · Confidence as a signal

Five samples are a usable confidence signal; the verdict-word probability is not

When all five samples agree, the verdict is right 84%–90% of the time. When they split, it is right only 53%–63%. The probability on the verdict word adds nothing on top. After the judge has reasoned, that probability is almost always near 0 or 1. Confidence also does not track human doubt: judges give a unanimous verdict on about 78% of the answers the experts split on, about as often as on clear ones.

4 · Asking the judge to say when it is unsure

Offered an UNSURE option, the judges do not use it

We repeated the real-answer runs with one change to the prompt: the judge could answer VERDICT: UNSURE when it could not tell and the answer should go to a human expert. The judges chose it on at most 0.2% of answers, including the ones they then got wrong. The five-sample vote does the job instead: sending split votes to a human catches a third or more of each judge's errors.

5 · What the reasoning looks like

A confident miss reads exactly like a confident correct verdict

A blind coder described the reasoning behind confident errors and an equal number of confident correct verdicts. When the judge wrongly accepts an answer, its reasoning recomputes just as often and hedges just as rarely as when it is right. Its wrong rejections recompute less and argue about definitions.

6 · Inside small judges

Small judges know more than they say, and the gap shrinks as they grow

Four open models saw the same grading prompt with the verdict forced right away, and a linear probe read their hidden state at the verdict position. In the 0.8B models the probe beats the model's own verdict by +0.24 to +0.30 AUROC on planted errors; at 4B–9B by only +0.06 to +0.08. Jeff, the decision-trained 0.8B model, says no more than its base but holds a slightly cleaner signal. A probe for "the experts disagreed on this answer" stays near 0.6 in every model.

Do small models see what the big judges miss?

No. Among answers all experts marked wrong, the ones a strong judge confidently accepted are also the ones small models find most convincing. Values above 0.5 mean the small model shares the blind spot.

7 · Over-strict judges?

Most confident rejections of approved answers point at real flaws

With the reference, the judges' remaining confident disagreement goes the other way. In 61 cases a judge rejected, with all five samples, one of 33 answers that every expert had marked correct. 11 of those answers were rejected by all three judges, and 35 of the 61 cases are gemma-2-2b answers. A coder that saw the judge's reasoning agreed with the judge every time, so we ran it blind. It saw only the question, the reference and the answer, and we compared against answers all judges had accepted.

The expert "correct" label often accepts an answer whose conclusion is roughly right while a step, definition or fact is wrong; the judges grade the whole answer. A second judge or a split vote would rarely overturn these rejections, and should not. The one exception is Qwen3.6, where another judge accepts 43% of its rejections at a 9% escalation rate. The four cards were checked by hand; the other answers still need a human check before we quote a label-error rate.

What this means

  1. Planted numeric slips are not where strong reasoning judges fail. Their confident failures without a reference are method and premise errors they share with the answer models.
  2. A reference fixes most of those. With a reference, the remaining confident disagreements are mostly cases where the expert label is too lenient, so judge accuracy against BFF's unanimous labels is understated.
  3. For a router: escalate when the five samples split, and always supply a reference when one exists. A self-reported UNSURE, the verdict-word probability, hedging language and item features add almost nothing.
  4. Hidden-state probes help mainly for very small judges, whose verdicts lag what they hold. They do not see the big judges' blind spots, because small models share them.

Limits

  • One answer set (BFF-Bench). Planted errors: 50–125 per type. DeepSeek-V3.1 was withdrawn by Tinker mid-run, so its planted and UNSURE results are partial.
  • The coder is an LLM. The 33 disputed answers need a human check (about 1 hour).
  • Probed models answer without reasoning and are at most 9B, so they show the mechanism and do not describe gpt-oss-120b. Best-layer probe numbers are slightly optimistic.
  • Planted errors were written by DeepSeek-V3.1, which is also one of the judges; its detection rates are not higher than the others'.
