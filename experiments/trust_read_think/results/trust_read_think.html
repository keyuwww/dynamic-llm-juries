Trust, Read, Think: when should an AI judge stop and think?

A judge can answer on gut, we can read its hidden state with a probe, or it can reason first. We tested which works where, and built a cascade that picks per item.

Judges: Qwen3.5-4B, gpt-oss-20b, Nemotron Nano 9B · 5 benchmarks, 1,659 graded responses · Nov 2026

Bottom line. The best read depends on the task. On recognize tasks (is this response safe, on topic, factual?) a probe on the hidden state beats both the gut and thinking, with no thinking cost, after only ~50–100 labels. On solve tasks (is this math or finance answer right?) only thinking helps.

The method: train a probe on ~100 labelled items, use its verdict, and think only on the 20% of items where it is least sure, ranked across all tasks together. For all three judges this beats thinking on every item at a fifth of the cost (gpt-oss: 0.839 vs 0.803), and it sends thinking to math by itself.

Earlier version: a cascade that calibrates gut and probe and thinks on 20% of each benchmark beats the standard gut → think escalation for all three judges (+2.9, +7.0 and +10.9 points), at the same cost. A jury of the three, thinking on 20% of items, reaches 0.837: at least as good as the best single judge and above a jury that thinks on everything (0.819).

The three reads

All three come from the same judge. The first two come from one forward pass, so they cost the same; only Think costs tokens.

TrustThe gut verdict: thinking off, the next word after “VERDICT:”.
ReadA linear probe on the hidden state at that same word. Needs labels to train.
ThinkReason first (up to 1,024 tokens), then give the verdict.

Why Read: in the earlier weight-space study the 0.8B judge “knew” the right answer inside (probe 0.95) but said it at chance (0.51), and editing the weights to fix that was blunt. Here we read the knowledge instead of editing it.

What we did, step by step

  1. 0
    Pilot

    Can a small judge think without looping?

    Qwen3.5-0.8B loops on 78–98% of items. Switched to Qwen3.5-4B with a thinking budget (forced verdict at 512 / 1,024 / 2,048 / 4,096 tokens).

  2. 1
    Three reads

    Record the gut verdict, hidden state and thinking verdict for every item.

    1,359 items on Safety, Focus, Math and BFF. Saved once, so every later step runs on a laptop.

  3. 2
    Which read wins

    Compare Trust, Read and Think on each benchmark.

    Read wins on recognize tasks (Safety 0.95, Focus 0.97). Think wins on solve tasks (Math 0.93). A probe trained on other benchmarks does not transfer.

  4. 3
    Free routing signals

    Does anything free predict where thinking changes the answer?

    Gut uncertainty does (AUROC 0.70–0.86). The probe adds nothing to it.

  5. 4
    Read without labels

    Train the probe on the model's own confident or agreed answers.

    Fails: self-made labels copy the gut's mistakes.

  6. –
    Literature

    Read 10 probing papers and their code.

    All train probes on gold labels; none measures how few labels a judge probe needs.

  7. 5
    Literature probes

    Six published probes × 25 to all labels, with a length control.

    25–50 labels already beat the gut on recognize tasks, and the gain survives the length control. No probe helps on Math.

  8. 6
    Held-out benchmark

    Run everything on RewardBench 2 Factuality, unseen until now.

    Read with 50 labels beats the gut and thinking (AUROC 0.90 vs 0.86). A per-benchmark read-or-think test is too noisy.

  9. 7
    Cascade

    Route each item: gut, probe, or think.

    Sequential gut → probe failed on Math. Cascade v2 (more confident of gut/probe, think on the rest) wins overall.

  10. 8
    Staged / longer thinking

    Peek 512 tokens before a full think; give thinkers 2,048 tokens.

    No gain at equal token cost. Kept the simple cascade.

  11. 9
    Confidence intervals

    Paired bootstrap over questions for every comparison.

    The cascade's gain over gut → think holds on average (CI above 0).

  12. 10
    Two more judges

    Repeat everything with gpt-oss-20b (OpenAI) and Nemotron Nano 9B (NVIDIA).

    Same split: Read wins on recognize tasks for all three. Their gut verdicts are overconfident, which broke cascade v2.

  13. 11
    Calibrated cascade

    Calibrate the gut on the same labelled items before comparing confidences.

    Fixes it: the cascade now beats gut → think for every judge (+3 to +11 points).

  14. 12
    The jury

    Three judges, each with three reads, voting.

    A dynamic jury that thinks only on the 20% of items where the jury is least sure beats thinking on everything.

  15. 13
    Task-format probe

    Predict recognize vs solve from the hidden state, on a benchmark it never saw.

    Spots recognize tasks almost perfectly but misses Math. Routing on format adds at most ~1 point.

  16. 14
    Pool the thinking budget

    Think on the least confident 20% of all items, not 20% per benchmark.

    Thinking moves to Math by itself (~40% of Math items). +0.8 to +1.7 points for every judge; Math gap about half closed.

6 · The jury

Each of the three judges gives a calibrated free verdict (gut or probe, whichever is more confident). The dynamic jury sums the judges' confidence and sends only the 20% of items where the jury is least sure to thinking, for every judge. Other juries are shown for comparison.

7 · Spend thinking where it helps

What the pooled version does. Put every item from every task in one pile, rank them by how unsure the probe is, and think only on the 20% it is least sure about. The probe is often unsure on math, so math gets most of the thinking; safety and focus barely get any, and do not need it. No one tells it which tasks are hard.

Before this, every benchmark got the same 20% of thinking, even though thinking hurts on recognize tasks and helps on solve tasks.

  • Math improves significantly for every judge (+2.4 to +5.3 points) and no benchmark gets significantly worse. About half of the math gap closes (gpt-oss 0.75 → 0.80, against 0.88 thinking on everything), at no extra cost.
  • A task-format probe (step 13) would route budget the same way but misses Math on held-out tests; knowing the true format adds only ~1 more point.

What did not work

  • Read without labels. Probes trained on the model's own confident or agreed answers learn its mistakes.
  • One probe for all benchmarks. Trained on three benchmarks, it scores below the gut on the fourth.
  • Picking which Math items to think on. Perfect routing would reach 0.86 at 16% thinking (Qwen); every per-item signal we tried (gut confidence, probes, a learned router, a short peek) stays near 0.76. Giving math more of the budget (section 7) helps more than picking items better.

What this adds

  • A per-item gut / probe / think cascade for LLM judges, compared with escalation and a learned router at equal cost. Each piece exists in the literature; we found no paper combining them for judges.
  • The task-format split: hidden states replace thinking on recognize tasks, not on solve tasks.
  • The first label-efficiency curve for judge probes: ~50–100 labels per benchmark, and none is not enough.
  • A dynamic jury that spends thinking only where the whole jury is unsure, across three model families.
  • Pooling the thinking budget across tasks by probe confidence: it allocates thinking to solve-type tasks by itself.

Closest prior work: probes on hidden states as judges (Representation-as-a-Judge, ICLR 2026; Calibrating LLM Judges, 2512.22245), probe-based compute allocation across queries (Learning How Hard to Think, 2410.04707; DiffAdapt), and confidence-based escalation (FrugalGPT; Think When Needed, 2601.18146; JEV-as-a-Judge, 2609.26550). The techniques are known; what is new here is the evidence on judges: when reading beats thinking, how few labels it needs, and that Read → Think with a pooled budget beats standard escalation for three model families.

Limits: 300–459 items per benchmark; three judges. The cascade rule was revised twice after seeing results (v2 after Math, gut calibration after the new judges), so Factuality and the two new judges are the cleanest tests of the first and second revisions respectively. Code and numbers: experiments/trust_read_think/ (steps 1–12; results per judge in results/<judge>/, the jury in results/jury/). Pooling assumes a judge sees a mix of tasks to rank together.
