Escalation Signals for LLM Judges

A cheap judge grades every answer. A signal gives each verdict a trust score, and the 20% least-trusted verdicts are sent to a stronger judge, whose verdict replaces the cheap one. This page compares signals by how accurate the final verdicts are.

Setup

  • Cheap judges: Qwen3.5-4B, Qwen3.5-9B, gpt-oss-20b. Belief tests use the two Qwen judges.
  • Stronger judge: majority vote of the 3 best other judges in the 8-judge pool (BFF, Safety); Qwen3.6-35B (RB2 Math); another hosted judge (JudgeBench).
  • Benchmarks: BFF (459 finance answers), RewardBench 2 Safety (1,800), RB2 Math (about 630 answers, clean labels), JudgeBench (620 pairs).
  • Main number: accuracy at 20% = share of final verdicts that match the human label after escalating the 20% least-trusted. Answers the judge did not finish are always escalated first.
  • Significant: the 95% confidence interval of a difference, resampling whole questions, lies entirely above zero. Each test's pass/fail rule was written down before running it.
  • Baselines: random escalation; “correct”-first rule: uses only the verdict, no signal: every answer the judge called “correct” is escalated before any it called “incorrect” (order within each group random). The judge is wrong more often when it says “correct”, so this already beats random and is the bar a signal must clear; the judge's own confidence (probability of its verdict word); 10 reruns at temperature 0.7 (about 10 times the cost).

Summary of results

  1. Activation probe: the all-layer probe (logistic regression on each layer of the judge’s activations, averaged) beats the “correct”-first rule by 2.0 to 3.0 points in 5 of 6 BFF/Safety settings (significant). It needs no extra generation. It beats 10 reruns on Safety but not on BFF.
  2. Belief rule: the judge answers the question once itself; verdicts that disagree with its own answer are escalated first. On RB2 Math it beats the judge's confidence by 8.3 and 10.6 points (significant); on BFF it beats the probe by 3.0 and 3.1 points (significant, 7 solves).
  3. Zero labels: the belief rule works without any training and transfers between BFF and RB2 Math (3 of 4 settings significant).
  4. Scope: the belief rule works only when the question has a checkable answer (finance, math). On JudgeBench and Safety it gives no gain; the probe is the better signal there.

Significant over the “correct”-first rule (5 of 6)

Method

  • Input: the cheap judge's activations at the last token, right after it writes its verdict, at every second layer.

2 · Belief rule

Significant on BFF and RB2 Math

Method

  1. The cheap judge answers the question itself once, without seeing the candidate answer.
  2. It grades the candidate as usual.
  3. Disagreement: it says "correct" but its own answer differs from the candidate's, or it says "incorrect" but its own answer matches.
  4. Disagreeing verdicts are escalated first, then the rest in random order. No labels, no training, no activations.

Answer matching uses GLM-5.3-Flash. The rule follows the team's finding that judges approve answers that match their own guess, even when the answer is wrong.

3 · Belief works on a new benchmark without its labels

Significant in 3 of 4 settings

  • Labels: the human's right/wrong mark for each answer. A method that must learn from labels needs them collected for every new benchmark, which is costly.
  • The question: if a method is set up on one benchmark, does it still work on a different one, with none of that benchmark's labels?
  • Three versions of belief are compared. Each one sorts the grades using the match between the judge's own answer and the candidate's answer:
    • Learned on the same benchmark: a small logistic regression learns from that benchmark's labels how much to trust each grade.
    • Learned on the other benchmark: the same model, learned on RB2 Math and used on BFF (or the reverse). No labels from the benchmark it is used on.
    • Fixed rule: nothing learned; grades that disagree with the judge's own answer go first (section 2).
  • Result: all three score about the same, so belief carries over to a new benchmark. The probe does not: learned on other benchmarks, it drops toward or below the “correct”-first rule (table below).

4 · On math, flipping can replace the stronger judge

Math only

Flip: a verdict whose belief score says it is more likely wrong than right is reversed, with no call to a stronger judge.

Cheap judge aloneEscalate 20% to the stronger judgeFlip, no stronger judgeQwen3.6-35B grading every answer
  • RB2 Math: flipping alone gives 92.5 (4B) and 93.9 (9B), above escalating 20% and above the 35B judge.
  • BFF: flipping alone gives only 79.6 (4B, from 76.0) and 78.4 (9B, from 77.8); escalation is still needed.
  • Pre-set test (escalate and flip vs escalate only) passed in 1 of 4 settings, so this is not a general result.
